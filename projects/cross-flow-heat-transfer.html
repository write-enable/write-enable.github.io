Fall 2025 ME 103

Tip effects in cross-flow heat transfer

Textbook convection formulas assume a part is infinitely long, which ignores heat lost through its ends. We measured how much that matters for short aluminum bars cooled by a fan, and compared against simulations of an infinitely long bar.

Team
Wen Cao, Athul Krishnan, Arundhati Parikh, Thomas Yu
Trials
18, across 3 lengths and 2 air speeds
Tools
FLIR thermal camera, hot-wire anemometer, Ansys Fluent, MATLAB

The question

Many real parts, like the fins of a PC cooler, are short, so the heat leaving through their tips is not negligible. We wanted to measure the gap between infinite-length theory and real finite-length parts.

Setup

We cut black-anodized 6061 aluminum bar, 12.7 × 6.35 mm in cross section, into 50, 100, and 150 mm samples, three of each. Each one was heated on a hot plate, hung by insulated wire in a 3D-printed rig, and cooled by two PWM-controlled fans while a thermal camera recorded its temperature against a white card backing.

We calibrated fan speed against duty cycle with a hot-wire anemometer, giving 0.7 m/s at 25% and 1.6 m/s at 62.5%.

Simulation

A 2D transient simulation in Ansys Fluent stands in for an infinitely long bar, since it has no ends at all. It used the k-omega SST turbulence model, a refined mesh around the bar, and the measured air speeds as inlet conditions.

Finding h

For every second of data, we used the bar’s temperature drop and Newton’s law of cooling to back out the convection coefficient h. This relies on the bar being the same temperature throughout, which we checked with the Biot number: about 0.003, far below the 0.1 threshold.

h is in W/m²K. Each measured value averages 600 samples from three trials.

In every case the measured h was higher than the simulation, and a two-tailed z-test showed the difference is statistically significant at the 5% level. But the gap shrinks steadily as the bars get longer, so real parts converge toward infinite-length theory, as expected. Uncertainty propagation showed thermal-camera noise dominated every other error source by several orders of magnitude.

Along the way

We started with round bars, but their curved surface threw off the thermal camera by up to 2 °C, so we switched to flat rectangular ones. Raw aluminum reflected too much for accurate readings, so we used black-anodized stock with a white backing. And a PWM fan controller replaced a DC supply to keep air speed consistent between trials.

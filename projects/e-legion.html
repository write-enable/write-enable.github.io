Fall 2025 ME 139

E-LEGION passive walker

A four-legged walker that steps down a ramp using only gravity, with no motors or control. Inspired by the geometric movement of TARS from Interstellar, we designed a knee that locks and unlocks on its own, and modeled how mass placement shapes the gait.

Team
Wen Cao, Yankang Liu, Jazlyn Peraza, Alyn Panggabean, Ziteng Ji
Result
Two full passive steps
Tools
3D printing, MATLAB symbolic dynamics

Background

Passive dynamic walking shows that stable, humanlike walking can come from mechanics alone. McGeer’s 1990 work showed this on a shallow slope with no actuation, Tedrake and colleagues showed how large curved feet support 3D passive walking, and Hsu Chen’s thesis showed that knees help foot clearance but must lock before the heel strikes, or the leg collapses. That last point turned out to be exactly what went wrong in our early builds.

Prototype 1: straight legs

Our first build had no knees. It could only move forward with stepping stones and could not walk down a continuous ramp, because the legs had no clearance to swing. It did teach us how much swing and stance timing matter.

Prototype 2: a knee that locks

Next we added a four-bar linkage knee that swings freely and then locks into an L shape during stance, making the leg rigid at full extension. It still fell forward: the crescent feet scrubbed the ground, there was no added mass for momentum, and all four knees tended to bend at once. Knee stoppers on the middle legs let it stand on the slope, but not walk.

Prototype 3: walking

Small tungsten weights near the knees helped the swinging legs reach full extension on time, and extra weight on the rear legs added a little drag that staggered when each leg locked. We redesigned the feet with a pointed toe to clear the ground and equalized the shank lengths. With slope and surface friction tuned with masking tape, the walker took two full steps for the first time.

Modeling the dynamics

We derived the Jacobians for the thigh and shank and built the full Lagrangian equations of motion, with mass matrix, velocity terms, and gravity, symbolically in MATLAB. This let us see how leg length and mass distribution change the joint accelerations.

What’s next

The gait was still sensitive to friction, slope, and starting pose. Next steps would be testing lighter weights and other mounting points, trying compliant or adjustable knee locks, reshaping the foot with a shorter heel and swappable friction pads, tightening print tolerances so all four legs match, and adding a release rig so every trial starts the same way.
